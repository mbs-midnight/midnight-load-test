Load test findings · Midnight preview & stagenet

Filling Midnight Blocks

We set out to hold Midnight preview at 50% block fullness and measure whether DUST fees respond. We reached 4.3% of the byte budget — and, measured afterward on the dimension that actually binds, 32% of the state-write budget in our fullest block. Chasing why we saw no fee movement led somewhere more useful: mainnet has been at the DUST fee floor since its second hour of existence, and the newer ledger already fixes it — which we verified by driving a block past the crossover and watching the price move.

preview: midnight-js 4.1.1 · ledger-v8 8.1.0 stagenet: midnight-js 5.0.0-beta.7 · ledger-v9 1.0.0-rc.3 24 wallets · 16-core host

Scope of the claim

We did not demonstrate that Midnight cannot scale. We demonstrated that a single well-resourced client cannot drive it hard enough to find out. Under our peak load the chain showed no strain at all: block cadence held at 6.000s across 499 intervals, 100% of blocks accepted our transactions, and every well-formed transaction that reached consensus succeeded. We peaked at 23 transactions in a block. For that transaction shape the ceiling is about 72 per block, set by state writes (696 B each against a 50,000 B budget), not the ~260 that bytes on the wire would suggest.

Three findings since change the picture. First, the fee price is a per-block state variable which falls at every fullness we achieved, so the experiment was pushing the number we wanted to raise in the wrong direction — and on mainnet it has been pinned at MIN_COST for 2.39 million blocks, making every transaction cost exactly 1 SPECK. Second, the newer ledger generation already fixes this via a min_block_price floor set to the genesis price; we confirmed the adjustment works by putting one stagenet block at 56.9% fullness and measuring the rise. Third, for transfers and contract deployments bytes_written — not bytes on the wire — is the dimension that gates block fullness; bytes on the wire bind contract calls and large shielded transfers; compute, read time and storage churn never bind at all. That changes what filling Midnight blocks costs, and it means our own load test was closer to the 50% crossover than the byte proxy showed.

Mainnet has been at the DUST fee floor since block 1,197

overall_price is a per-block state variable that scales every DUST fee. It rises when blocks are more than half full, falls when they are not, and is clamped below at MIN_COST. Decoded from per-block ledgerParameters on indexer.mainnet.midnight.network (ledger 8.1.2, node 1.0.2):

height          date          overall_price
     -     genesis       10                        INITIAL_PARAMETERS
     1     2026-03-18     9.102091156410182        = 10 x 0.9540488^2
   479     2026-03-18     0.0000000015627278225258633
 1,197     2026-03-18     0.000000000000000005421010862427522   <- MIN_COST
   ...
2,393,873  2026-09-01     0.000000000000000005421010862427522

The descent took 1,197 blocks — about two hours, and the floor has held for every one of the 2.39 million blocks since, sampled at seventeen heights across five and a half months. Block 1 is already two maximum-decay steps below genesis (10 x 0.9540488² = 9.102091156410184 against 9.102091156410182 measured), which is positive evidence the mechanism runs exactly as specified — the problem is not that it is broken but that its equilibrium is the floor.

Realised fees follow mechanically. Over a 6,001-block window, all 126 fee-paying transactions cost exactly 1 SPECK — a single distinct value. Preview (also ledger-8) reached MIN_COST by block 1,324 and has held it for ~660,000 blocks; all 1,861 transactions from our load test there also cost exactly 1 SPECK.

At the floor no transaction that can physically exist bills more than 1 SPECK. Because the fee is (… x overall_price x SPECS_PER_DUST).ceil(), reaching even 2 SPECK would need a transaction roughly 61 completely full blocks in size. So the floor destroys not just the fee level but the fee schedule: a contract deployment and a dust-sized transfer cost the same.

What the floor costs an attacker

The specification (spec/cost-model.md) frames overall_price as denial-of-service deterrence: "an attacker has to pay as much as a regular user." At the floor that defense can be priced. Assumptions: NIGHT at $0.02, 5 DUST of capacity per NIGHT refilling in about a week (measured: 5,000 NIGHT accrued 3,639 DUST in ~24 h, a 6.87-day refill), and the adjustment live and compounding at +4.5951% per fully saturated block, which is what we measured. Because the climb is exponential from a base eighteen orders of magnitude down, almost the whole cost of an ascent back to the genesis price falls in its last hundred blocks:

An hour of complete saturation is free to three decimal places. The mechanism bites eventually — at two hours it is ruinous — but not inside the window an attacker would choose, and the window renews itself: rise and decay rates are near-symmetric, so stopping at 859 blocks and waiting 972 blocks for the price to fall back to the floor gives a repeatable 1,831-block cycle.

Roughly $0.32 per day holds 47% of all mainnet blocks at 100% fullness, indefinitely. The duty cycle is fixed by the ratio of the climb rate to the decay rate, so it is the same at any attack amplitude; and because the gap being exploited is fifteen orders of magnitude, the conclusion is insensitive to the token price — at $0.50 the same attack costs $7.99 per day. The attacker's binding cost is proving compute, not DUST; the economic defense is not weak so much as absent.

The floor's depth is not something the specification chose. Its update() applies a MIN_COST clamp to the four per-dimension factors, which are normalized around 1, where it is a rounding guard that "still allows upwards adjustment"; overall_price receives no floor at all in the spec. The implementation applies the same constant to overall_price, which starts at 10 — turning a rounding guard into a floor eighteen orders of magnitude down. Ledger 9's min_block_price is the fix for exactly that.

The newer ledger fixes this, and we verified it

Stagenet runs a different parameter generation. Its ledgerParameters blob is tagged [v8] where mainnet and preview are [v5], and the structural diff between them is exactly one field:

+ min_block_price    (absent on v5)    FixedPoint(10)   on stagenet

Its value is 10 — precisely the genesis overall_price. So the floor has been raised from MIN_COST (5.42 × 10-18) to the genesis price, and stagenet's price rests on it rather than collapsing eighteen orders of magnitude.

The 10 is the price of one full block, not a minimum per transaction. A transaction pays for the fraction of a block it consumes, so at the floor a light contract call that uses 2% of a block costs about 0.20 DUST and a plain transfer 0.26–0.55 DUST. State writes are priced at four times the rate of the other dimensions (write_factor 2.28 against 0.57), which is why a contract deployment can cost 13.8 DUST while using only 59% of the write budget.

Correction. An earlier revision of this report concluded that the fee adjustment was inert on ledger-9, from a run that raised block occupancy to 27.6% and saw no movement. That conclusion was wrong, and wrong for an avoidable reason: every test was below the 50% crossover, where the specified behavior is decay — and a price resting on a floor is indistinguishable from a frozen price when you only ever push it downward.

Above the crossover it moves, and it tracks the specified curve

Harvested from stagenet's own traffic

Rather than generate every data point, we scanned 6,000 consecutive stagenet blocks and recovered the fullness the node reported, by taking the per-block multiplier as price N / price N-1 and inverting the curve. Using the ratio rather than assuming a baseline of 10 keeps it correct when high-fullness blocks land consecutively and the price is compounding.

54 of 6,000 blocks crossed the 50% threshold — about one in 111, entirely from organic traffic plus our three. A recurring contract deployment is strikingly deterministic: the same 13.537 DUST shape produced a multiplier of 1.003429574649, identical to twelve digits, across roughly thirty separate occurrences.

Why the fee experiment could not have worked

This is the central finding, and it was prompted by an observation from the explorer: stagenet transactions average ~1.22 DUST, while every preview transaction we ever submitted cost 1.000 SPECK. We decoded both chains' live ledgerParameters to find out why. The answer is a single scalar.

Every factor agrees. Only overall_price differs — and it is a plain linear multiplier on the entire fee (cost_model.rs:400: self.overall_price * (utilization_cost + write_cost + churn_cost)).

Preview's value is not arbitrary. 5.421010862427522e-18 = 100 × 2-64, which is FixedPoint(100) — and MIN_COST is defined as exactly FixedPoint(100). Preview is pinned precisely on the floor. Our 1,861 transactions at 1.000 SPECK were reading that floor, not a broken fee market.

The price is a per-block state variable, not configuration

It is recomputed every block from the previous block's fullness, then clamped:

overall_price ← max(overall_price × (1 + logit(fullness)/a),  MIN_COST)

price_adjustment_function(usage, a) = -ln(1/clamp(usage, 0.01, 0.99) - 1) / a      // a = 100

That gives a per-block multiplier bounded by 0.954 (empty) and 1.046 (full), crossing 1.000 at exactly 50% fullness. The consequence for this experiment is stark:

Three things follow. The 50% target was a real cliff, not a soft one — below it the price actively falls, so no amount of runtime at 12% fullness could ever have produced a reading. The mechanism is fast once above it: a detectable 2× move takes 15 blocks at high fullness, about 90 seconds. And the mechanism is not broken — stagenet, which is priced 1.8×1018 higher, charges real fees. We measured a live 51,809-byte transaction there at 1.80 DUST under chain parameters, consistent with the ~1.22 DUST explorer average.

The practical reading for MNF: a chain that has been idle long enough sits at MIN_COST and stays there. Fee-response testing must begin by driving fullness above 50% and holding it — the absolute fee level before that point carries no information, on any network, for any duration.

Transaction economics

Measured on the wire, every transaction type lands in the same order of magnitude of bytes per unit of proving. But bytes on the wire are only one of five dimensions, and for transfers they are not the one that binds — see the next section. Which dimension actually binds

Overall fullness is the maximum across five normalized dimensions, so the binding one decides everything — and it is not the one we assumed. We downloaded transactions from high-fullness stagenet blocks, rebuilt them locally with ledger-v9, and computed cost(params) against the chain's live parameters:

The decomposed bytes_written figure reproduces the node-reported fullness exactly — 58.490% against the 58.4909% recovered independently by inverting the price curve. So bytes_written, capped at 50,000 bytes and 20× tighter than block_usage, is what gates fullness for contract activity. A contract deployment consumes 58% of a block's write budget while using 2.8% of its byte budget. Compute is not close: 0.27%.

We then extended the decomposition to every regular transaction in the last 6,000 blocks of all three networks — 1,334 transactions, 3 September 2026 — each rebuilt with the matching ledger library and costed against its own network's live parameters. block_usage binds 748 of them (contract calls, large shielded transfers), bytes_written 575 (transfers, deployments), compute_time 11 (small contract calls whose three cheapest dimensions sit within a hair of each other). read_time never exceeds 4.7% of a block and bytes_churned never exceeds 1.5%; neither ever binds. At the ledger level Midnight is neither CPU-bound nor I/O-bound. The full shape-by-shape capacity analysis is in the companion page, Midnight's Capacity Ceiling.

Applied to our own preview load test, the same decomposition shows what the byte proxy hid. Our fullest block, 637,940, held 23 fallible-segment transfers writing 696 B each: 8.4% of the byte budget but 32.0% of the write budget, with compute at 12.3%. Our mean of 8.6 transactions per block was about 12% write fullness. The 50% crossover needed roughly 36 such transfers in one block, against a measured single-client ingest ceiling of about 11.

Efficiency therefore depends entirely on which resource is scarce, and there is no single "best" attack or load shape:

Shielded transfers are 1.7× more DUST-efficient at producing fullness, because the fee sums max(read, compute, block) + write + churn while fullness takes the maximum — so a write-heavy deploy pays for writes that do not raise its fullness, and a block_usage-heavy transfer is "full" cheaply. Where DUST is effectively free, as at the mainnet floor, many-output unshielded transfers win outright: a 16-output transfer is 15.7% of the write budget for 1.6 s of proving, so four of them cross 50% for about 7 s of proving per block.

Shielded scaling — and what it implies for a 50% run

With shielded working, we measured how it scales. Stagenet's block limits, decoded live, are block_usage 1,000,000 B, bytes_written 50,000, bytes_churned 50,000,000, and read_time / compute_time of 2.000 s each. Overall fullness is the maximum across all five, so the binding dimension is what matters, not the byte count.

block_usage binds throughout, and the marginal cost of a shielded output is 6,793 bytes and 681 ms of proving. That gives the number this whole exercise was looking for:

A single 32-output shielded transaction is 26.5% of a block. Two of them clear the 50% crossover. Extrapolating the marginal cost, one transaction of ~65 outputs reaches 50% on its own, at roughly 47 s of proving — verified: our 68-output transfer, 569,482 B, was 56.9% and proved in 56 s.

This reframes the ceiling. Our measured single-client mempool limit is 1.9 tx/s, about 11 transactions per 6-second block — and 50% fullness now needs two. The mempool ingest limit, which capped the plain-transfer approach at about 15% write fullness, is no longer the binding constraint. What replaces it is proving throughput: sustaining two 32-output transactions per block means ~47 s of proving per 6 s of chain time, or roughly 8 concurrent proving lanes.

It also removes the faucet from the critical path. A minting contract issues arbitrary amounts of a shielded token to arbitrary keys, so a fleet can be bootstrapped for a shielded flood without a faucet and without any NIGHT-shielding step — which is fortunate, since that step does not work in practice today.

Defects

Resolved Error 170 on shielded transfers is a wallet-sdk 1.x defect, fixed on ledger-9

On preview, wallet-sdk 1.1.0 and 1.2.0 fail every shielded transfer with 1010: Invalid Transaction: Custom error: 170 (MalformedError::InvalidDustSpendProof), 3/3, while unshielded transfers from the same wallets succeed 1,861/1,861. Shielded tokens sent from Lace into those same wallets arrive correctly, which established it as a client defect rather than a protocol one.

It does not reproduce on the 2.0 stack. Four shielded transfers, four successes, verified on chain. This closes the investigation and explains Lace: Lace is on the newer client line.

The comparison is like-for-like, which we checked rather than assumed. Both sides move a user-created shielded token, not native NIGHT: the preview wallets hold token 1f1a2c72… (Lace-sent) and the stagenet wallet holds 55dd57fb… from a contract we deployed. Both are derived tokenType(domain_sep, contract_address) hashes. Native NIGHT could not have been the variable in either case — NIGHT swaps fail on both networks, see the initSwap card below.

Consequence: no fix is needed on the ledger-9 path, and no 1.x workaround should be carried forward. For anyone still on ledger-8, shielded remains unusable from the SDK. Verified on: wallet-sdk 2.0.0-beta.2 + midnight-js 5.0.0-beta.7 + ledger-v9 1.0.0-rc.3 + proof-server 9.0.0-rc.5.

Medium initSwap with NIGHT fails on every network, for two different reasons

Moving NIGHT from the unshielded pool into the shielded pool is rejected by the node every time we tried. A small transaction returns 231 (FeeCalculation(OutsideTimeToDismiss)); a larger one returns 199 (InvariantViolation::NightBalance). Reproduced 6/6 across TTLs of 1 h / 10 min / 5 min, output counts 1 / 4 / 8, and 5.4× transaction padding.

Reviewer feedback (Midnight architecture, September 2026) corrects our original reading. There is no design intent to forbid swapping NIGHT, which is why the API offers it. The practical obstacle is the guaranteed section: a swap places the NIGHT offer there, and NIGHT transfers of almost any shape cost more to validate than the per-byte time-to-dismiss allowance permits, so the transaction is refused before it is ever applied. That is the 231 we saw on small transactions, and it is why padding the transaction with cheap bytes came closest to getting through.

The 199 on larger transactions is a separate check that we think still deserves a look. check_night_balance_invariant in ledger/src/semantics.rs requires

utxo_ann.value + locked_pool + reserve_pool + block_reward_pool
    + treasury_night + unclaimed_rewards + contract_value  ==  MAX_SUPPLY

and none of those terms is the shielded pool, so a transaction that does clear the guaranteed section and moves NIGHT into Zswap would reduce utxo_ann.value with nothing to compensate. The public midnight-ledger main (last commit 2026-03-30) still reads this way. If NIGHT swaps are meant to work, the invariant needs a Zswap term; if they are not, the API is offering something the ledger will always reject. Either way, the check dates to 6.1.0-alpha.2 (2025-09-05), so no NIGHT has ever been shielded on preview or stagenet, and every shielded balance on these networks is a user-created token.

Suggested: confirm which of the two outcomes is intended. In the meantime the SDK lets a caller build, sign, prove and submit a NIGHT swap the chain will refuse, paying the proving cost, and the 231 it gets back points at fee calculation rather than the cause. A client-side guard or a documented "not currently possible" would save the next team the same hours.

Blocker wallet-sdk@2.0.0-beta.2 does not install

wallet-sdk-facade@5.0.0-beta.2 does import { Clock } from '@midnight-ntwrk/wallet-sdk-utilities', but the barrel pins wallet-sdk-utilities to 1.2.0, which predates Clock. A clean install of the exact version set in the partner document fails at import time, before any user code runs:

The requested module '@midnight-ntwrk/wallet-sdk-utilities'
does not provide an export named 'Clock'

Workaround: an npm override to 1.2.1, whose exports are a strict superset. Already fixed upstream: the newest v2 canary, 2.0.0-canary.20260730145109-1cf94a3, pins utilities@1.2.2-canary and installs and imports cleanly with no override. Fixed in 2.0.0-beta.3 (published since this was first written): it pins utilities@1.2.2-beta.0 and a clean install imports without an override — verified with repro/01-wallet-sdk-beta2-install.sh against beta.2 and a fresh stagenet-next/ install of beta.3.

High wallet-sdk@2.0.0-beta.3 cannot sync an unshielded wallet against the indexer stagenet runs today

Re-running the reproductions on the newest published stack (wallet-sdk 2.0.0-beta.3, midnight-js 5.0.0-beta.8, ledger-v9 1.0.0-rc.4) stopped at the first transaction: every attempt failed with Wallet.InsufficientFunds for NIGHT on a wallet holding 5,000 NIGHT. The unshielded wallet had never connected. Its subscription is rejected by the indexer:

Wallet.Sync: Unknown field "protocolVersion" on type "UnshieldedTransactionsProgress".

The beta.3 indexer client (wallet-sdk-indexer-client 2.0.0-beta.2) asks for protocolVersion on every progress update. That field was added to the indexer by midnightntwrk/midnight-indexer #1463 on 2026-09-02 and is in no indexer release tag as of 2026-09-14; stagenet's indexer serves UnshieldedTransactionsProgress { highestTransactionId } only. The shielded and dust subscriptions still sync, so the wallet reports itself connected on two of three sub-wallets and simply has no NIGHT. beta.2's client does not request the field and works.

Porting the harness to beta.3 also met three unannounced API changes: ShieldedWallet(c).startWithSecretKeys(keys) and DustWallet(c).startWithSecretKey(key) are gone in favor of epoch-forking startWithSeed(seed) / startWithKeys({ v8, v9 }); facade.start(zswapKeys, dustKey) became facade.start(WalletSeeds.fromMasterSeed(seed)); and transferTransaction / initSwap dropped their secretKeys argument, so the old three-argument call silently treats the keys object as options. The facade state is now { protocolVersion, state } per sub-wallet.

Suggested: either deploy an indexer with #1463 wherever beta.3 is meant to be used, or have the client tolerate a schema without the field. Until then the newest beta cannot transact on stagenet, which also means the wallet-side defects above could only be re-verified on beta.2. What we could check statically on beta.3: the facade still calls submitTransaction(tx, 'Finalized') and ledger-v9 rc.4 still takes the enforcement flag as optional. Reproduction: stagenet-next/repro/diag-state.ts.

Medium Client-side fee checks: the enforcement flag is off by default, and older tooling prices from genesis

The flag. In ledger-wasm/src/tx.rs, both cost() and fees() resolve their enforcement argument as enforce_time_to_dismiss.unwrap_or(false), so the time-to-dismiss check the node applies is off by default in the binding every wallet uses. Reviewer feedback is that this is worth filing and is very likely already fixed. We could not confirm the fix from source: the public midnight-ledger repository has not moved since 2026-03-30, while ledger-v9 1.0.0-rc.4 and rc.5 shipped on 2026-08-11 and 2026-09-14 from a tree we cannot see. The rc.5 JavaScript binding still passes the flag through as optional, which tells us nothing about the compiled default. We also tried to show the default mattering in practice and could not: on every NIGHT swap we can build today, cost(params, true) passes just like cost(params), and the node then rejects the proven transaction with 199 rather than the 231 we saw in August, so no current transaction distinguishes the two settings. Filed as a question, not a confirmed defect.

The model. LedgerParameters.initialParameters(), the genesis defaults compiled into the SDK, is not what any network runs: diffed against live parameters it disagrees in 143 lines on stagenet and 142 on preview, and on one live transaction the same fee came out at 4.92 DUST against genesis and 1.80 DUST against the chain. On the wallet-sdk 2.0 line this is already handled: the dust wallet deserializes ledgerParameters from synced block data and prices fees from that, using initialParameters() only as a fallback for the dust grace period. The exposure is now confined to tooling that calls initialParameters() directly, which included our own ledger-8 harness.

Suggested: confirm the default of the enforcement flag in the current ledger tree, and note in the SDK docs that initialParameters() is a genesis constant, not a fee-estimation input.

High The latest proof-server tag is two ledger generations stale

midnightnetwork/proof-server:latest resolves to 7.0.0-rc.1, a ledger-7 build. Against a ledger-8 SDK it produces valid proofs but at 0.53 proofs/s. The correct image, midnightntwrk/proof-server:8.1.0, manages 5.2 proofs/s — a 9.8× difference, or 14.2 versus 1.17 core-seconds per proof.

The two organisation names differ by two characters — midnightnetwork versus midnightntwrk — and the stale one holds the latest tag. Nothing warns you: proofs verify and transactions land, just an order of magnitude slower.

Suggested: retire or repoint the stale tag, and have the SDK warn when the proof server's /version does not match the ledger generation it was built against. This single misconfiguration invalidated three of our intermediate conclusions.

Retracted "A single refused WebSocket permanently retires a wallet"

An earlier revision reported that one refused indexer WebSocket connect was unrecoverable, because wallet-sdk-indexer-client constructs graphql-ws with shouldRetry: () => false. That line is real and still present on midnight-wallet main (2026-09-14), but it is not the layer that matters. Reviewer feedback said the SDK has retry capabilities; we tested it rather than argue. A throwaway wallet was pointed at a local WebSocket endpoint that (a) accepts and closes immediately, (b) rejects the upgrade with HTTP 403, as the rate limiter does, and (c) proxies to the real indexer for 20 s and then drops and refuses. In all three cases every sub-wallet reconnected on an exponential schedule — 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 s after the failure, on beta.2 and again on beta.3 — and the source (RunningV1Variant.js, Schedule.exponential(1 s, 2), jittered, capped at 2 minutes) shows it does not give up. The same schedule is present in the dust-wallet 4.1.0 that wallet-sdk 1.1.0 pinned, so it was there during our preview runs too.

What we actually observed — two wallets at zero throughput for six minutes after a 403 — is consistent with the rate limiter's block simply outlasting the run while the reconnects kept failing, not with an absent retry. progress.isConnected did read false throughout, which is the observable state we said was missing.

What remains: nothing is surfaced as an error or event while reconnection is in progress, so a caller has to poll isConnected to know the wallet is degraded, and once the backoff reaches its 2-minute cap a recovered endpoint is noticed up to 2 minutes late. Both are ergonomics, not defects. Reproduction: repro/README.md, B3.

High Dust cold-sync is prohibitive and degrades with concurrency

A cold dust sync takes minutes per wallet and worsens sharply in parallel. Two wallets synced in 446 s; six concurrent produced one usable wallet in 49 minutes and the rest hit a 45-minute timeout. Priming 24 wallets was not achievable in a working day.

The sub-wallets expose serializeState() / restore(), which solves it completely — we measured warm-up dropping from ~386 s to ~9 s, a 6.5× improvement with no loss of correctness — but this is not documented as the intended path and no example uses it.

Suggested: document snapshot/restore as the standard way to operate more than one wallet, and investigate why concurrent cold syncs collapse rather than merely slow down.

Medium Single-client mempool ingest saturates near 2 tx/s

A six-step rate ramp found 1016: Immediately Dropped — the transaction couldn't enter the pool because of the limit first appearing at 2 tx/s offered. Achieved throughput peaks at 1.88 tx/s and then degrades as offered load increases.

The curve does not merely plateau — it rolls over. Throughput peaks at 1.88 tx/s at 3 offered, then declines to 1.80 and 1.68 as offered load rises, while pool rejections climb from 72 to 128. Past saturation, additional offered load makes the system deliver less. That is congestion collapse rather than a clean ceiling, and it means a client tuned by pushing harder will land in a worse place than one tuned by backing off.

196 (InvalidError::DustDoubleSpend) rises in lockstep with offered load — 14, 52, 63, 77 across the ramp. Two in-flight transactions from one wallet select the same dust coin because the first spend is not yet reflected in wallet state. It is a client-side race, but it is provoked by the same pressure, and it compounds the ceiling.

Open question for MNF: is this limit per-connection, per-peer, or global? It caps single-source load at roughly 11 transfers per block — 4.3% of the byte budget, about 15% of the write budget for the shape we used — regardless of client resources. If it is per-connection, distributed submission bypasses it; if global, no client can fill blocks alone. We could not determine this from outside, and it is now the single most important unknown — with shielded working on ledger-9, it is the remaining barrier to a 50% run.

Medium submitTransaction hardcodes Finalized

The facade always waits for finality. Measured, that is ~19 s of a ~23 s cycle — the dominant cost for a load generator that does not need it. The underlying service accepts 'Submitted' | 'InBlock' | 'Finalized', but the public method exposes no parameter. Reaching past the facade cut our cycle 6×, from 23 s to 3.8 s.

Suggested: expose the wait mode on the public API. Still present on wallet-sdk 2.0.0-beta.2 — stagenet submits also cost ~18.6 s.

Medium signRecipe is required for transfers and breaks registrations

Unshielded transfers must call signRecipe before finalizeRecipe; omitting it yields Custom error: 192 (InputsSignaturesLengthMismatch). But registerNightUtxosForDustGeneration and deregisterFromDustGeneration already sign internally, so calling signRecipe there double-signs and produces the same error code 192 from the opposite cause. All seven of our registrations failed this way.

Suggested: distinct error codes for missing versus surplus signatures, and documentation of which recipes are pre-signed. finalizeRecipe proving but not signing is a sharp edge that costs hours to find.

Medium Errors are catch-alls that hide the cause behind an Effect symbol

"Transaction submission error" and "Failed to prove transaction" carry no diagnostic content. The real cause — the node's rejection code — sits several levels down a cause chain, and for anything through runPromise it hangs off a symbol rather than .cause, so ordinary unwrapping walks straight past it.

Every substantive diagnosis in this exercise required writing a custom unwrapper first. Once we had one, error 192 was identified in minutes.

Suggested: surface the ledger error code on the thrown error.

Medium The indexer computes block fullness and discards it

ledgerParameters is exposed only as opaque hex, so external tooling cannot read the real block limits — which is precisely how the stock 200,000-byte default goes unnoticed while the chain runs at 1,000,000, and how a fee price sitting on MIN_COST goes unnoticed entirely. The indexer already computes block_fullness internally and throws it away, leaving block_usage as the only externally derivable dimension of five.

Suggested: expose block_fullness, decoded limits, and overall_price. Every finding in the fee section above required decoding opaque hex with the ledger WASM to obtain numbers the indexer already holds.

Medium Rate limiting is undocumented and disproportionate

Measured thresholds: clean at ≤0.25 req/s serial, sporadic 403s from ~4.6 req/s, hard IP block at ~12 req/s sustained, recovering after 1–3 minutes of quiet. The block covers indexer, RPC and faucet over both HTTP and WebSocket.

Because sync runs over the WebSocket, a rate-limited IP cannot sync wallets at all. The wallets do keep reconnecting (see the retracted card above), but for as long as the block lasts every attempt is refused, so from the client a 1–3 minute IP block is 1–3 minutes of a wallet that looks idle.

Medium Undocumented breaks in the ledger-9 migration

Each of these cost time and none are in the partner document.

  • createKeystore now takes {kind, secret}, not raw bytes, and kind is an explicit 'schnorr' | 'ecdsa'. The wrong value silently derives a different address — a fund-losing default. 'schnorr' is the ledger-8-compatible choice.
  • Private-state passwords now require 3 of 4 character classes, not just length ≥ 16. Existing passphrases fail at deploy time with Found: 2.
  • The ledger package is published under two npm scopes. The 2.0 SDK betas depend on @midnightntwrk/ledger-v9 (no hyphen, unlike every other Midnight package), whose latest dist-tag points at 0.1.0-rc.1 from June even though 1.0.0-rc.5 exists, so npm i @midnightntwrk/ledger-v9 without a version installs a stale prerelease. The hyphenated @midnight-ntwrk/ledger-v9 only began publishing on 2026-08-10 (rc.3 onward) and carries the same rc.4 / rc.5.
  • Retracted: an earlier revision said proof server 9.x dropped --network. Running the binary inside each image shows the flag set is identical across 7.0.0-rc.1, 8.1.0 and 9.0.0-rc.5 (--num-workers --port --job-capacity --job-timeout --no-fetch-params --verbose) and none of them ever accepted --network; the flag came from our own launch script.
  • compactc 0.33.0-rc.2, the version the document specifies, was never published to the compact release channel — compact update 0.33 fails with No version matching 0.33 found. The lowest installable toolchain is 0.34.0 (runtime 0.19.0), which pairs with midnight-js 5.0.0-beta.7, not the document's beta.4.
  • New in 5.x: a Terms & Conditions flow (WalletFacade.fetchTermsAndConditions) returning a live hash and URL.

Suggested: publish a migration note and correct the version matrix. The one thing that is right and load-bearing: all three of wallet-sdk, midnight-js and the contract toolchain agree on ledger-v9 1.0.0-rc.3, so only one copy of the ledger WASM is installed. That was the failure mode on the ledger-8 line and it is worth stating explicitly.

What it would take to reach 50%

The fee curve crosses at exactly 50%, so this remains the minimum bar for the experiment to produce any signal — and the table above shows it must then be held, not merely touched.

  • Move to ledger-9. This supersedes "fix error 170". Shielded transfers work there, and a single 68-output shielded transfer clears the crossover on its own — one of two routes that sidestep the mempool ceiling.
  • The mempool question is no longer the blocker. It capped the plain-transfer approach at about 15% write fullness because 50% needed ~36 transactions per block against an ingest ceiling of ~11. A 68-output shielded transfer needs one; 16-output unshielded transfers need four. The open question of whether 1016 is per-connection or global still matters for other workloads, but it no longer stands between us and a 50% run.
  • Use write-heavy shapes, not plain transfers. The cheapest is a many-output unshielded transfer: 16 outputs reach 15.7% of the write budget for 1.6 s of proving, so four per block cross 50% with ~7 s of proving per 6 s block — roughly two proving lanes and 0.67 tx/s of submission, both comfortably inside every limit we measured. A 28 KB contract deployment is 58% on the same dimension but needs 1.71 tx/block and about 7 proving lanes; a 68-output shielded transfer is 57% but needs 56 s of proving each.
  • Staging a batch needs one NIGHT UTXO per transaction. Pending dust is tracked per DUST UTXO, and each NIGHT UTXO backs exactly one DUST UTXO, so a wallet can pre-prove as many transactions as it has NIGHT UTXOs whose own dust covers a fee (verified on stagenet: six UTXOs, six pre-proved transactions). Holding N consecutive blocks above 50% therefore needs N funded UTXOs, in one wallet or several; the balancer selects the smallest coin first and sweeps crumb UTXOs into every build, so the UTXOs should be equal-sized.
  • Budget for the hold, not the peak. Reaching 50% is not the end of the experiment. At 60% fullness the price moves 0.41% per block; a convincing reading needs sustained load for hundreds of blocks, not a burst.
  • Wallet operability. Snapshot/restore should be the documented default; without it, fleet operations are impractical.

The most useful correction in this exercise: our first capacity estimate was 310 CPU cores for 50% fullness. That figure was entirely an artifact of the stale proof server. The real number is 26. A single wrong container tag moved the projected cost of this experiment by more than an order of magnitude.

What we did not establish

  • Whether the chain sustains high fullness. We never exceeded 32% on any dimension in a block of our own, and the 57–59% blocks on stagenet were single transactions. Block production was metronomic throughout and showed no degradation.
  • Whether the price compounds over many consecutive blocks. We measured the single-block multiplier at three fullness levels and matched the specified curve to five significant figures, and the source multiplies rather than re-derives — so 10 → 13.2 over 100 blocks at 56.9% is arithmetic on a verified factor. But we never held fullness for more than one block at a time, because that needs a funded NIGHT UTXO per block.
  • Whether mainnet's floor is intended. It has held since block 1,197 of 2,393,873, which is either deliberate or something nobody has had reason to look at. The remedy exists in the [v8] parameter line; whether it is scheduled for mainnet is a question for the engineering team.
  • Whether the mempool limit binds the network. We measured one client against one node.
  • Why 231 appears at all on the swap path, given the underlying cause is a supply invariant. Two different checks reject the same illegal transaction depending on its shape.
  • What a full block costs a validator. We have since decomposed all five cost dimensions for 1,334 transactions, so the binding dimension is no longer a blind spot. What remains unmeasured is node-side: block production and validation time, propagation, and state growth under sustained full blocks. Every capacity figure here is ledger accounting, not a measurement of the node under load.
